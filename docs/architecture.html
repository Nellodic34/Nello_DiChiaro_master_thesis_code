Two Eyes on One Drone: How the Vision Stack Turns Two Bearings Into a Trajectory

A walkthrough of the detection, triangulation and tracking pipeline used to estimate the state of a target drone from two bearings-only observer drones, fused with an IMM (CV/CA) filter and graded offline against RTK ground truth.

Outline

Two drones, observer1 and observer2, each carry an RGB-D camera and watch a third drone, the target, whose position and velocity are otherwise unknown to the system. Neither observer alone can tell how far away the target is — a camera only gives a direction, a bearing. What makes the target's 3D position observable is having two of them: as long as both observers can see the target at (nearly) the same instant, the two bearing lines meet, or nearly meet, at a single point in space. Everything described below exists to make that meeting point as accurate, as fast, and as robust to noise and false detections as possible.

Concretely, the story runs like this. Each observer, independently and onboard its own drone, turns its camera image into a clean, timestamped bearing ray in a shared world frame — that is the whole job of observer_node.py, and it involves a detector, a pixel-space tracker, a coordinate transform, and a couple of defensive filters that keep the ray honest. Both observers then broadcast that ray as a small binary packet. A third node, the fusion node (triangulation_node.py, running onboard observer1), listens to both streams, matches up the rays that belong to the same instant, triangulates a 3D point, and feeds it into an IMM filter that tracks the target's position and velocity through two different motion models. What comes out the other end — an odometry estimate, a predicted trajectory, and a set of RViz markers — is then either watched live on a ground-station PC, or graded offline against RTK ground truth once the flight is over.

2

The Observer

observer_node.py runs identically on both drones, configured only by a role parameter (observer1 or observer2) that picks the right image topic and camera extrinsics. Its job stops well short of "where is the target in 3D" — that question is deliberately left to the fusion node. What it must deliver, every frame, is a single honest measurement: a ray from the camera's known position, in a known direction, in the world frame, together with a confidence and a timestamp precise enough to be matched against the other observer's ray later. Getting there takes seven stages, each building on the last.

Read this before §2.6–2.7: it is tempting to assume Observer Self-Rejection and the Tight-Coupling gate act on the already-smoothed track, late in the pipeline. They do not. Both are candidate filters that run inside extract_best_detection(), per raw YOLO box, before the pixel-space Kalman filter's own confirmed update() call happens in process_image(). The order per candidate, read directly off the source (extract_best_detection() in nodes/observer_node.py), is: class filter → OSR veto → KF Mahalanobis pre-gate (or ghost gate) → Tight-Coupling gate → depth consistency gate (only when depth_fusion.gating_enabled=true). Only detections surviving all of that are candidates for choose_best_candidate() to pick from.

2.1  Seeing the target: camera and detector

Everything starts from the RGB-D camera's compressed color stream (vision.main_image_topic for observer1, vision.observer_image_topic for observer2) and its matching CameraInfo, from which the node reads the intrinsic matrix K. The callback image_callback() in nodes/observer_node.py does nothing but buffer the latest frame; the actual work runs on a fixed-rate timer, processing_timer_callback(), which hands the frame to process_image(). That function calls self.model.predict(...) — a YOLO/OpenVINO detector loaded from vision.model_path in config/runtime_stack.yaml, running on CPU on the real hardware (vision.device) — which returns bounding boxes with a confidence score.

2.2  Keeping the crop tight: ROI-adaptive detection

Running the detector on the full frame every time is wasteful once the target has been found once. So the node keeps a region of interest (ROI) around the last predicted position and only expands back to a full-frame scan periodically (roi_full_scan_interval, every 20 frames) or when the track has been lost for longer than roi_track_timeout_sec (0.5 s). A track is only allowed to arm the ROI once a handful of recent detections agree with each other: at least roi_init_m of the last roi_init_n candidates (3 of 5) must fall within roi_init_max_spread_px pixels of one another. The crop itself grows by a roi_margin_multiplier (1.40×) around the predicted center and is rejected outright if it would shrink below roi_min_size_px, which stops the ROI silently starving itself at lower stream resolutions.

2.3  Smoothing the pixel track: the 2D Kalman filter

Raw YOLO boxes are noisy and occasionally wrong, so a class named ImageSpaceKalmanTracker (nodes/observer_node.py) tracks the detection in pixel space before anything is turned into a 3D ray. It is used twice per frame, not once: first as an approximate Mahalanobis pre-gate (evaluate_gate()) applied to every raw candidate inside extract_best_detection() — this is stage 3 in Fig. 2 — and again as the real predict+gate+update cycle (update()) applied only to the single winning detection back in process_image() — stage 4. The parameter kf_n_confirm, declared under kalman_filter: in config/runtime_stack.yaml and read at node startup, sets how many consecutive hits promote a track from TENTATIVE to CONFIRMED; only a CONFIRMED track is allowed to reach back-projection (§2.4). kf_nis_threshold and kf_nis_window control a separate rolling consistency check (is_nis_consistent) that only logs a warning about a possible persistent false positive — it does not by itself reject anything. A track surviving a short occlusion for up to kf_ghost_timeout_sec is allowed to re-acquire through a dedicated M-of-N ghost re-entry gate rather than the normal init gate. Camera ego-motion compensation (kf_ego_motion_enabled) keeps the pixel-space prediction valid while the observer drone itself is rotating, by rotating the predicted state with R_ego, computed once per frame in process_image() from two TF lookups.

Feedback loop: the predicted center coming out of this filter is exactly what feeds back into §2.2 to place next frame's ROI window — the pixel tracker and the ROI cropper close a small loop around each other, frame after frame.

2.4  From pixel to bearing: back-projection into the world frame

Once the KF's confirmed track produces a filtered pixel center (x_filtered, stage 4), compute_and_publish_ray() in nodes/observer_node.py back-projects that filtered center — not the raw YOLO box center — through the inverse of K into a camera-frame direction vector, then rotates it into the world frame using two pieces of information: a static extrinsic transform from the flight controller (FCU) to the camera mount (measured once per airframe, declared under camera_tf: in config/runtime_stack.yaml), and the drone's own live RTK pose (its position and attitude in the world). The result is a ray: an origin (the camera's world position) and a unit direction (the bearing). If the exact-time transform isn't available, the same function can fall back to the latest known transform within max_tf_delta_ms (40 ms, declared under vision:) — a decision recorded in the packet itself (see §2.8) so the fusion node knows how much to trust it.

2.5  Adding range: optional depth fusion

depth_fusion in config/runtime_stack.yaml has one master switch, enabled, but it feeds two distinct roles that are easy to conflate:

  • Range annotation (this section): inside compute_and_publish_ray(), whenever enabled=true and a valid depth reading exists under the bbox, _extract_depth_from_bbox() samples the aligned depth image (median filtering, per Khoshelham & Elberink 2012) and the packet is upgraded from V1 (bearing-only) to V2 (bearing + range) — see §2.8.
  • False-positive rejection (stage 3 in Fig. 2, inside extract_best_detection()): only runs when the second switch, depth_fusion.gating_enabled, is also true, and rejects a candidate whose measured depth is inconsistent with the IMM's predicted range along the ray (_check_depth_gate(), gated at gate_k sigma).

On the current hardware, depth_fusion.enabled is disabled (so is gating_enabled): the USB link carrying the RGB-D stream was downgraded from USB 3.0 to USB 2.1 to remove RTK interference, and that link no longer has the bandwidth for an aligned depth stream — the comment above depth_fusion: in the YAML is explicit that leaving it true anyway would make preflight_check.py fail for no benefit.

2.6  Not shooting the wingman: observer self-rejection (Layer 4)

Two drones flying a coordinated formation will, sooner or later, fly into each other's field of view. Without a safeguard, an observer would happily report its sibling drone as if it were the target. Observer self-rejection fixes this — and, per the note after Fig. 2, it acts early: inside extract_best_detection(), before any candidate reaches the pixel-space Kalman filter's confirmed update. Each observer subscribes to the other observer's own odometry (/{other_uav}/estimation_manager/odom_main), projects that sibling's position onto its own image plane using the pinhole model — gate_px = (rejection_gate_radius_m / Zc) · max(fx, fy), exactly as documented above observer_self_rejection: in the YAML — and vetoes only the single nearest in-gate raw detection (a Global Nearest Neighbor veto, not a blanket disc rejection, so an unrelated distant detection sharing the same bearing is never collaterally discarded). The gate radius comes from rejection_gate_radius_m (0.8 m, the sibling's physical body radius); max_odom_age_sec makes the whole layer fail closed if the sibling's odometry goes stale. This layer is enabled by default on real hardware — unlike every other optional feature described in this document.

This is a genuinely different feedback path from the tight-coupling gate below: it carries the sibling observer's own pose, not the fused target estimate, and it exists purely to suppress a specific, known false-positive source.

2.7  Listening to the fusion node: the tight-coupling feedback gate

The observer can optionally close the loop the other way, through a second candidate filter in the very same extract_best_detection() pass (immediately after the OSR veto and the KF pre-gate, per Fig. 2 stage 3): it subscribes to the fusion node's own /target/odometry_estimate and forward-extrapolates it to the current frame time (p(t) = p(t₀) + v·Δt + ½a·Δt², computed once per frame by _get_tc_projection()), then projects that predicted 3D point back onto its image plane to build a χ²-gated ellipse tested by _is_inside_tc_gate(). Detections that pass the pixel-space Kalman pre-gate but fall outside this 3D-informed gate are rejected before ever reaching the confirmed KF update. Two safety switches keep this from backfiring, both read from tight_coupling: in the YAML: the gate disables itself automatically if the 3D estimate's covariance trace exceeds max_3d_covariance_trace, or if the estimate is older than max_3d_age_sec — otherwise a temporarily wrong 3D estimate could "swallow" a perfectly good detection and never recover. This entire layer is disabled by default (tight_coupling.enabled: false).

2.8  Packing it up: the observation packet

Everything above collapses into one compact binary payload — packed by pack_observation_packet() / pack_observation_packet_v2() in nodes/observer_node.py, a fixed-layout struct rather than a JSON blob, to keep bandwidth predictable — carrying the image timestamp, an observation id, the confidence, the camera's world-frame origin, the bearing ray, the age of the TF used, and a flag marking whether that TF was an exact match or a fallback. A second, slightly larger packet variant adds a valid-depth flag, the range, and its sigma when depth fusion is active (§2.5). This packet is published on /{uav_name}/target_observation, and that topic is the entire interface between an observer and the fusion node — nothing else about how the ray was produced crosses that boundary.

The Fusion Node

triangulation_node.py runs onboard observer1 and receives both observers' observation packets. Where the observer's job was to turn pixels into one honest ray, the fusion node's job is to turn two rays, arriving asynchronously from two independent clocks, into a single filtered belief about where the target is and where it is going.

3.1  Matching rays across two cameras: predictive ray pairing

Before two rays can be triangulated, the node has to decide which observation from observer1 actually corresponds to which observation from observer2 — trivial with one target in frame, ambiguous the moment there are decoys, reflections, or multiple candidate detections. The entry point is try_match_and_triangulate() in nodes/triangulation_node.py, which repeatedly calls _select_best_observation_pair() to drain both observation buffers pair by pair. When triangulation.pairing_prediction_enabled (declared in config/runtime_stack.yaml) is true, that selection runs a Global-Nearest-Neighbor association implemented in score_prediction_guided_pair() / PredictionGuidedPairScore, minimizing a composite cost rather than simply picking the closest timestamps:

J = w_time·(|Δt| / max_pair_age_sec) + w_geom·(e / max_triangulation_error_m) + w_innov·(d² / pairing_mahalanobis_chi2)

Δt is the timestamp gap between the two observations, e is the residual gap between the two 3D rays at their closest approach (from triangulate_midpoint()), and d² is the Mahalanobis innovation of the resulting midpoint against the IMM's current prediction. The three weights — pairing_weight_time, pairing_weight_geom, pairing_weight_innov, all declared under triangulation: and read once at node startup — let the pairing trust temporal synchronization, ray geometry, and filter consistency together rather than any one of them alone. Setting pairing_prediction_enabled: false instead falls back to select_timestamp_only_pair(), the legacy min-|Δt| rule.

3.2  Turning two rays into a point: triangulation

A matched pair of rays is triangulated by triangulate_wls() (nodes/triangulation_node.py) as the closest point between two skew lines in 3D, weighted least squares (WLS) under an Errors-In-Variables model so that the observers' own pose uncertainty can (optionally, observer_odom_uncertainty.use_odom_uncertainty) be folded into the solution rather than assumed away. Because the closed-form WLS solution is a pseudolinear estimator — it minimizes a metric residual, not the true angular one — it carries a small asymptotic bias that does not vanish as more measurements accumulate. The optional ekf.ml_refine flag corrects for this with up to ekf.ml_refine_iters (5) Gauss-Newton iterations on the true angular residual (refine_triangulation_gn()), started from the WLS answer and falling back to it if a step ever diverges.

ekf.normalize_crlb is not a no-op. Older docs called it one; the rescaling branch had been deleted once and is now restored at the end of triangulate_wls(). true rescales R to trace = 3·kf_measurement_noise; false (current) keeps the raw CRLB R = Λ⁻¹. Either way it changes the covariance handed to the IMM, and preflight_check.py prints which branch is active.

3.3  Modeling how the target moves: the IMM estimator

A single motion model is always a compromise: constant-velocity (CV) is smooth and appropriate for hover or straight flight but lags during a sharp turn, while constant-acceleration (CA) reacts quickly to maneuvers but is noisier the rest of the time. The IMMFilter class in nodes/triangulation_node.py runs both in parallel — each backed by its own LinearKalmanFilter built from build_transition_matrix_cv()/build_process_noise_cv() and build_transition_matrix_ca()/build_process_noise_ca() — and blends them, once per frame, inside predict_and_correct(), through a 2×2 Markov switching matrix self.pi, so the filter leans on whichever model currently explains the target's motion better.

3.4  When one eye closes: the single-ray fallback

Mutual occlusion happens: one observer may lose line of sight to the target while the other keeps tracking. When no valid pair exists but one observer's unpaired ray carries a valid depth measurement and a YOLO confidence of at least triangulation.single_obs_min_confidence (0.60), the single-observation fallback (execute_single_observation_fusion() in nodes/triangulation_node.py) accepts a one-dimensional range update along that single ray rather than coasting blind. This path requires depth_valid on the incoming packet — it is a graceful degradation that only exists at all because depth fusion (§2.5) can, when enabled, still supply a range even with one eye closed; with depth_fusion.enabled=false as on the current hardware, a single-eyed occlusion simply coasts (§3.5).

3.5  What comes out

The fused belief is published as a full nav_msgs/Odometry on /target/odometry_estimate — position, velocity, and covariance — alongside a lighter PoseStamped, a forward-looking Path on /target/predicted_trajectory, and RViz MarkerArrays built by _publish_position_markers(). A /tracker/fusion_mode string, written by _publish_fusion_mode(), reports how the latest estimate was produced: STEREO (a triangulated ray pair), SINGLE_DEPTH (the one-ray fallback of §3.4) or COAST (_coast_predict() extrapolating because no accepted measurement arrived within coast_after_sec). It does not report which IMM model dominates. When ground truth is available, publish_ground_truth_error() additionally reports the live error against it. It is this same odometry topic that the observer's tight-coupling gate (§2.7) subscribes back to — the one true feedback edge from the fusion node back to the sensing stage.

4

Evaluation & Monitoring

4.1  Grading the prediction against the truth

evaluate_prediction_node.py is a ROS node that listens during the flight (or a bag replay) and compares the predicted trajectory against the RTK ground truth once both have been aligned into the same frame (the target's raw odometry lives in its own origin, whose z-datum can differ from the observers' by several metres — comparing the two directly would just chart that gap as tracking error). It writes everything on shutdown (Ctrl-C), into debug/evaluation/<timestamp>/ with debug/evaluation/latest pointing at the newest run: prediction_eval.json, a per-sample CSV, a markdown report and the plots (*_plot.png, *_xy.png, *_xyz.png, *_distance.png, *_est_vs_odom.png). With launcher.drone_run_mode: decentralized_evaluation the triangulation launcher starts it automatically on observer1, so the folder appears on that machine. The report contains:

  • ADE — Average Displacement Error across the whole predicted horizon
  • FDE — Final Displacement Error, the error at the end of the prediction horizon
  • Position & velocity RMSE
  • Distance to the observers at the moment the prediction was made

4.2  Watching it live

live_monitor.py runs on a ground-station PC on the same ROS graph over WiFi and plots ground truth, estimate, prediction, and both observers' own positions in real time, in one of three layouts:

Repository & Launch Flow

Putting the whole story back into motion on real hardware takes three processes on the two drones, plus an optional fourth on the ground:

ScriptWhat it starts
launch_observer_detection_node.shobserver_node.py with role observer2
launch_main_detection_node.shobserver_node.py with role observer1
launch_triangulation_node.shtriangulation_node.py (fusion + IMM)
launch_live_monitor.shthe 3D monitor, on the ground-station PC

In the typical flow, observer2 runs its own observer_node.py, observer1 runs both its own observer_node.py and triangulation_node.py side by side, and live_monitor.py optionally joins from a PC on the same network. Full setup and troubleshooting details live in README.md.

Two facts of the network that shape the config. The drones talk over Zenoh, which does not carry /tf_static between them. So (1) the fusion node cannot bridge the two drones' world_origin frames through utm_origin; evaluation.shared_world_origin: true declares them identical instead, and that only holds if their Z datum really agrees (see README, Preflight). (2) Anything that needs uav8's camera frames on uav11 cannot work: the uav8 debug overlay stays empty, and uav8's ROI re-acquisition around the 3D estimate fails silently, so uav8 re-acquires the target more slowly than uav11 after a loss.
